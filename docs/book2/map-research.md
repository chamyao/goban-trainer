# Map research: what makes an RPG map fun to walk

This is research for the Places design (`places-design.md`). It covers what Pokémon, Zelda, Nintendo's stage design, stealth games and Chinese RPGs teach about laying out towns, routes and buildings. Each lesson ends with what it means for *Hulao Pass*.

---

## 1. Pokémon: routes and towns

- **Routes are short and do one thing.** A route links two towns and adds one idea: tall grass, a ledge, a bridge of trainers. Nugget Bridge (Route 24) is a narrow bridge with five trainers in a row, and you have to beat each one to cross.
- **Ledges are one-way shortcuts.** You can hop down but not climb back up, so the way forward is fast and the way back takes the long road. Route 4's ledge stops you walking back from Cerulean until later.
- **Trainers who see you walk over.** A trainer standing in a narrow pass is a roadblock the player understands at a glance.
- **Towns wait for their big event.** The next route opens after the town's main event, and the roadblock is always something in the story: a guard, a sleeping Snorlax.

**For us:** routes should be short, each with one feature. Challengers stand where the path narrows. A roadblock is always someone or something from the novel.

## 2. Zelda: one path that feels open

- **Most of the time there is one critical path, hidden inside branches.** Side rooms come off the path, so the player feels they chose their own route.
- **Keys pull you off the path and back.** How far the key sits from its door sets the difficulty.
- **Self-contained.** Later Zelda games keep every key inside the dungeon where it's used.
- **Stealth in Ocarina's castle courtyard:**
  - It's a few small yards in a row.
  - Each yard has one readable pattern: a guard who never looks back, then one fast guard and one slow one, then a beam to walk along above the guards.
  - Getting caught throws you back out to the start of the courtyard. There's no game over.
- **The Forsaken Fortress (Wind Waker):** you have no sword, so you hide under barrels, and searchlights throw you in a cell.

**For us:** each beat has one path, with townsfolk and optional challengers branching off it. Legwork keeps its "keys" (pearls, crown, edict) inside the same place. Stealth works the way the courtyard does: small rooms, one pattern each, and a gentle reset when you're seen.

## 3. Nintendo's four-part stage (起承转合, kishōtenketsu)

Super Mario 3D World's director, Koichi Hayashida, describes each stage as showing one idea in four steps: **introduce** it, **develop** it, **twist** it, then **conclude** and drop it, in about five minutes. The structure comes from Chinese and Japanese poetry (起承转合).

**For us:** each place, and each arc, teaches one map idea in four steps and then drops it. In Chang'an the idea is **being seen**:
1. Introduce: one watcher on a street during the crown delivery.
2. Develop: at the curtain, be seen by one man and not the other.
3. Twist: the garden, where you must reach Lü Bu unseen and then are seen by the one man who mustn't see you.
4. Conclude: the North Side Gate, where everything is done in the open.

## 4. Readability and landmarks

- **Players should always know where they are.** Use strong shapes and silhouettes, and give each map one landmark visible from most of it. Disney calls this a "weenie": the castle at the end of Main Street that pulls you down the street.
- **Limit choices.** Don't put many exits on one screen, so the player can hold the map in their head.
- **Light leads.** Bright areas draw the eye; dark areas hide things or mean danger.
- **Keep detail low on the ground and high on people,** so the eye finds people and doors first (Brendon Chung on Team Fortress 2).

**For us:** every map gets one landmark you can see from most of it.
- Chang'an: Weiyang Palace on its plateau.
- Meiwu: its walls, "as high as Chang'an's".
- The road: the dust of the procession ahead.

Night beats light only the path.

## 5. Stealth readability

- **Mark of the Ninja:** draws each guard's sight as a visible cone, so the player sees exactly what is watched. It's a 2D game, like ours.
- **Patrols:** circular patrols are easy to read but dull. L- and U-shaped patrols watch a small area more actively.
- **Safety changes over time.** A spot that's safe now may be swept by a patrol later, and designers should check every hiding place against the patrols.

**For us:**
- Cones are always visible.
- Patrols are L- or U-shaped and pause where they visibly stop to talk.
- Every room has at least one hiding place that is always safe.
- Each room has one pattern and takes under a minute.

## 6. Chinese RPGs

### Chinese Paladin (仙剑奇侠传) and its mazes
- **Players love the Paladin mazes' mechanisms**, such as the lotus pads that carry you across water on Fairy Spirit Island, or hidden switches that open the path when you step on them.
- **They hate mazes they can't read.** The Lock Demon Tower (锁妖塔) is the most-cited complaint: "the buildings look alike, the big loop has almost no landmarks or chests, so you can't tell where you've been". One player in Paladin 3 described the colours of the blood pools and paths as nauseating. Enemies that keep interrupting you make you lose your bearings.
- **Rewards in dead ends turn players into lab rats.** A gcores essay argues they make players search every corner compulsively. A wrong path should be atmosphere, never loot.

**For us:** no mazes made of look-alike corridors. Each room in a compound has its own landmark (the pond, the curtain, the pavilion). Dead ends hold texture (a townsperson, a view), never something the player must collect.

### Writing townsfolk: the Paladin rulebook
A Paladin designer, 贝勒王, set down rules for townsfolk lines around 2003:
- **Five jobs:** build the world, hint at the system, deepen the story, explain the plot, and make the world feel real.
- **About half the lines are idle talk.** It is what makes the world feel lived in.
- **Hints:** spread a hint across several people rather than one obvious line.
- **Lines change as the story moves on.** Lines that never change are on the list of faults.
- **Placement:** important people stand still in conspicuous places: entrances, crossroads, notable buildings. People who stand still are remembered better than people who wander.
- **Telling people apart:**
  - a fitting name
  - a distinctive pose or action, such as one who always faces something
  - their own catchphrase or rhythm
  - speech that fits their status
- **Faults to avoid:**
  - "a thousand faces, one look"
  - lines with no purpose
  - repetition across people
  - places that can't be told apart

**For us:**
- Every place has townsfolk for each state of the story.
- About half the lines are just life in a city under Dong Zhuo.
- Each important person stands still at a gate or a crossroads, with one recognisable habit.

### The chapter loop (from `research.md`)
*Chinese Paladin* runs each chapter as town → small conflict → dungeon → boss → the party changes. *Xuanyuan Sword: Clouds of Han* has its heroes witness recorded events without changing them. *GuJian* and *Black Myth* tell one complete story per act.

**For us:**
- The Diaochan arc is one city told in several states (§7), plus one dungeon (the Chancellor's residence) and one road (to Meiwu).
- The party change is the handoff between protagonists.

## 7. Revisiting one place as the story changes

Pokémon towns and Paladin's Yuhang town change between visits (new people, a burned house), and returning to a familiar place that has changed is one of the strongest moments in an RPG. The novel keeps the Diaochan arc almost entirely inside Chang'an, so **Chang'an is designed as one map in states**: Dong Zhuo's capital, night, the day of the gate, the celebration, and the sack. Players learn its streets early, and the sack lands because they know them.

## 8. Real geography: Han Chang'an (汉长安城)

The archaeology gives us a layout:
- The city is an irregular rectangle. Its four walls run 6,000 m (east), 4,900 m (west), 7,600 m (south) and 7,200 m (north).
- **Twelve gates, three in each wall.**
  - North wall, west to east: 横门 **Heng Gate**, 洛城门, 厨城门.
  - East wall: 宣平门 **Xuanping Gate** (north), 清明门, 霸城门.
  - The other walls hold the rest.
- **The palaces fill the south**, on the raised Longshou plateau (龙首原): Weiyang (west) and Changle (east).
- **The East and West Markets lie north of Weiyang.**
- Main avenues have three lanes. The middle lane (驰道) is the emperor's alone, and officials and commoners walk the outer two.
- The Heng Gate leads north to the Wei River bridge (横桥) and on to the west, toward Mei county, where Meiwu stood. Dong Zhuo leaves for Meiwu by the Heng Gate in chapter 8, and the officials see him off there.

**For us:**
- Palace south and raised, visible from everywhere: the landmark.
- Markets north.
- Heng Gate in the north-west: the gate of every coming and going to Meiwu.
- Xuanping Gate in the east wall: Wang Yun's last stand.
- The three-lane avenue: the emperor's lane down the middle, which no one walks, and which Dong Zhuo's carriage rolls down on his last day.
