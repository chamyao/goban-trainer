# Storytelling principles for a walk-and-solve story game

_Editor's reference, written before reviewing Misaeng Book 1. Each principle is summarised in my own words, says why it
matters for a game made of short scenes in which the player walks between places and solves go problems at key moments,
and lists its sources. **[W]** marks general writing craft and **[G]** marks game-specific narrative design. Several
sources are secondary write-ups of talks or books; I've cited the most direct one I could find._

---

## Part A: General narrative and writing craft

### 1. [W] Causality: "but" and "therefore", never "and then"
Trey Parker and Matt Stone check their outlines by reading the words that join one beat to the next. If the honest word
is "and then", the story is just a list of events. The beat should happen *because of* what came before (therefore) or
*in spite of* it (but). A chain of causes makes a story feel inevitable, and it lets the audience guess ahead and care
about the guess.
**Why it matters here:** a book of 20-plus short beats, each split up by walking and a board, is always at risk of
feeling like a slideshow. Each beat's opening line should say what the last beat caused. The playtester's complaint,
"events are just getting thrown at me one after the next", is this failure exactly.
**Sources:** Parker and Stone, NYU writing seminar, 2014 (record at https://speakola.com/arts/tag/NYU); summary by David
Perell, "The But & Therefore Rule", https://perell.com/note/but-therefore-rule/ ; Kenn Adams's "story spine" (every
day… one day… because of that…), as passed on in Emma Coats's "Pixar's 22 Rules of Storytelling",
https://www.openculture.com/2013/03/pixars_22_rules_of_good_storytelling.html

### 2. [W] Every scene turns a value
Robert McKee's test asks what is at stake for the character at the start of a scene, whether it's going well or badly,
and where it stands at the end. If nothing has changed, the scene is a non-event. Something must move: safe to unsafe,
alone to accepted, hopeful to beaten.
**Why it matters here:** with roughly one beat per episode there's no room for beats that only set up mood. Each beat
needs a turn the player can feel, and the board is the natural place for it.
**Sources:** Robert McKee, *Story* (1997); "Do Your Scenes Turn?", McKee Story blog, https://mckeestory.com/do-your-scenes-turn/

### 3. [W] Scene and sequel: goal, conflict, disaster, then reaction, dilemma, decision
Dwight Swain splits fiction into two units. In a *scene*, the character wants something, meets opposition, and comes
out worse off. In a *sequel*, the character reacts, faces a dilemma, and decides, and that decision becomes the goal of
the next scene. More scenes speed a story up; more sequels let it breathe and make it believable.
**Why it matters here:** walking between places is a built-in sequel. If the narration at the end of a beat says what
he has decided, the walk becomes the decision being carried out, and the next beat starts with a goal.
**Sources:** Dwight V. Swain, *Techniques of the Selling Writer* (1965); "Scene and sequel", Wikipedia,
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scene_and_sequel

### 4. [W] Setup and payoff (Chekhov's gun)
Whatever the story points at should matter later, and whatever matters later should have been pointed at first. A
payoff with no setup feels like cheating; a setup with no payoff feels like noise. The payoff doesn't have to be a plot
turn. It can be a feeling, a character beat or a theme.
**Why it matters here:** in a short-scene game, an early prop (a glue stick, a locked cabinet) that returns is one of
the most satisfying things the player can notice, because they handled it themselves.
**Sources:** "Chekhov's gun", Encyclopaedia Britannica, https://www.britannica.com/topic/Chekhovs-gun ; Final Draft,
"How to Put Setups and Payoffs in Your Scripts", https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/how-to-put-setups-and-payoffs-in-your-scripts

### 5. [W] Promise, progress, payoff: tell the audience what kind of story this is, then show it moving
Brandon Sanderson argues that a story's opening makes promises about its tone and its central problem. The middle has
to show visible progress on that problem, and the ending has to deliver what was promised, not something else. Readers
forgive a lot if they can feel progress; a middle without it reads as drift.
**Why it matters here:** a countdown ("seven weeks to the PT") is cheap and powerful progress. But the book's ending has
to pay off the promise its opening made. If the opening promises "a failed prodigy finds a new board", the close should
land there.
**Sources:** Brandon Sanderson, "2025 Guide to Plot (Lecture #2)",
https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-guide-to-plot-lecture-2 ; Odyssey Workshop summary,
https://www.odysseyworkshop.org/?p=19170

### 6. [W] A character who wants something, in a situation with stakes
Kurt Vonnegut's rules include that every character should want something, even a glass of water, and that the reader
should have someone to root for. Stakes are what the character loses if they fail, and they have to be concrete and
kept in view. Stakes that are mentioned once and then dropped stop working.
**Why it matters here:** the player needs to know at every moment what the lead wants and what failing would cost, or
the boards are just puzzles.
**Sources:** Kurt Vonnegut, "Creative Writing 101", preface to *Bagombo Snuff Box* (1999), reproduced at
https://peacecorpsworldwide.org/?p=1790

### 7. [W] Start late; earn the backstory
Vonnegut also advises starting as close to the end as possible. A long run of backstory before the story's present
asks the audience to care about events before they care about the person. Backstory works best when the present needs
it: a memory set off by something happening now, or a fact revealed under pressure.
**Why it matters here:** a game's opening minutes are its onboarding (see 15). Five beats of narrated childhood before
the player does anything of their own choosing is a lot of runway.
**Sources:** Vonnegut, as above; Victoria Mixon, "3 Things to Know About Exposition & Telling",
https://victoriamixon.com/2012/04/02/3-things-to-know-about-exposition-telling

### 8. [W] Show the moments that carry emotion; tell the connective tissue
"Show, don't tell" isn't absolute. Telling is the right tool for skipping time and summarising the routine. But the
moments the reader is meant to *feel* (a death, a humiliation, a rescue) have to be dramatised, or the reader gets
the information without the experience. Exposition delivered as a list ("As you know, Bob…") stalls a story.
**Why it matters here:** the playtester's notes ("a father's death never told", "a rescue that didn't land because the
bullying wasn't shown") are both about emotional moments that were summarised or left out.
**Sources:** Michael A. Ventrella on "As you know, Bob" (an SFWA term for info-dump dialogue),
https://michaelaventrella.com/tag/as-you-know-bob/ ; io9/Gizmodo, "5 situations where it's better to tell than show",
https://gizmodo.com/5-situations-where-its-better-to-tell-than-show-in-your-2-5655794

### 9. [W] Suspense comes from information the audience has
Hitchcock's bomb under the table: if the audience doesn't know about the bomb, its blast is a few seconds of surprise.
If they watch it being planted, the same small talk becomes minutes of suspense. Dramatic irony (the audience knows
more than the character) turns ordinary scenes tense or funny. Hitchcock's rule was to inform the audience wherever
possible, unless the twist itself is the point.
**Why it matters here:** cutting away to what the lead can't see (the waybill on the lobby floor, the partner being
threatened at his plant) is a strength of this kind of story, as long as the player knows enough to feel the gap.
**Sources:** Alfred Hitchcock in François Truffaut, *Hitchcock/Truffaut* (1966), summarised at No Film School,
https://nofilmschool.com/alfred-hitchcock-and-francois-truffaut-explain-surprise-vs-suspense ; Grammarly, "Dramatic
irony", https://www.grammarly.com/blog/literary-devices/dramatic-irony/

### 10. [W] Point of view: whose story is it, and who does the deciding?
A story's protagonist should drive its key turns. When another character solves the hero's problem, the climax goes to
the wrong person. Moving away from the lead's point of view can work (see 9), but each move should have a reason, and
the lead should get back to the deciding role at the moments that count.
**Why it matters here:** in a game the controlled character *is* the point of view. Handing the player another
character means handing them the story's agency, so it should be for that character's own turn, not to solve the
lead's.
**Sources:** Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), on the hero being introduced through an action and carrying the
A-story, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Save_the_Cat!:_The_Last_Book_on_Screenwriting_You%27ll_Ever_Need ; adaptation
guidance on choosing one primary dramatic perspective (see 12).

### 11. [W] Introduce characters one at a time, with a hook, and keep names distinct
Readers mix people up when too many arrive at once, or when names look or sound alike. Introduce people in small,
memorable groups, each with a clear role and one vivid trait; avoid names sharing a first letter or syllable; and pick
one name for each character and stick to it.
**Why it matters here:** a player reading short lines between walks has less attention for a cast list than a novel
reader. Similar names are doubly hard in a language the player doesn't know.
**Sources:** Anne Mini, "Need your opening scene contain a cast of thousands?", https://www.annemini.com/?p=3182 ;
Writing-World, "The Name Game", https://writing-world.com/fiction-characters/namegame.shtml

### 12. [W] Adaptation is compression: pick the spine, then cut and merge to it
When a long serial becomes a shorter work, the adapter keeps the central characters, the main line and the subplots
that carry its theme, and then builds back out. Episodic serial structure (a fresh incident each week) has to be bent
into an arc with a shape. Cutting costs some depth; keeping everything costs clarity. Readers of the source can fill
gaps that strangers can't.
**Why it matters here:** one beat per episode means the source's episodic rhythm comes through untouched, unless each
beat is deliberately tied to the book's spine.
**Sources:** Author Learning Center, "Screenwriting: Adaptation Overview",
https://www.authorlearningcenter.com/marketing/book-to-screen/w/adaptations/1689/screenwriting-adaptation-overview---article ;
TV Tropes, "Compressed Adaptation", https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/CompressedAdaptation

### 13. [W] Closure: the reader fills the gap, but only one they can cross
Scott McCloud names the work a comics reader does in the gutter between panels: from two images, the mind builds the
action between them. Gaps engage the reader, but only if both sides of the gap are clear enough to bridge.
**Why it matters here:** the game moves from a webtoon (built on gutters) to beats with walks between them. A jump the
source's art made obvious can be impossible to cross in text.
**Sources:** Scott McCloud, *Understanding Comics* (1993), discussed by Christopher Gavaler, "Analyzing Comics 101:
Closure", https://hoodedutilitarian.com/tag/closure

## Part B: Games and RPG narrative design

### 14. [G] Ludonarrative harmony: the play should mean what the story means
Clint Hocking named "ludonarrative dissonance" for when a game's mechanics say one thing and its story says another (in
*BioShock*, a story about selflessness with mechanics that reward selfishness). When the player's action and the
story's meaning agree, each strengthens the other. When they disagree, players feel it even if they can't say why.
**Why it matters here:** this is the most important game-specific rule for this book. If the player *solves* a board
and the story says the hero *failed*, the player is told their success means its opposite. Each board's caption and
its win line must agree with what the story says happened.
**Sources:** Clint Hocking, "Ludonarrative Dissonance in Bioshock" (Click Nothing blog, 2007), discussed in University of
Pittsburgh Games, "Ludonarrative Dissonance: What it Meant and What it Means",
https://www.games.pitt.edu/ludonarrative-dissonance-what-it-meant-and-what-it-means/ ; Toby Fox on building
*Undertale*'s story out of its mercy-or-fight battle system, The Mary Sue interview, https://themarysue.com/?p=342934

### 15. [G] Align the player's goal with the character's goal
Narrative goals are the player's reading of a gameplay goal: what they think they're trying to do and why. When the
two line up, the player wants what the character wants. One practical note from game writers is that players care
about what affects their play, not what they're told to care about, and that cut-scenes work better for introducing
obstacles than for setting goals.
**Why it matters here:** each board's caption is the player's goal at that moment. It should name what *the character*
is trying to do in the scene, in words that fit the scene.
**Sources:** Rogelio E. Cardona-Rivera, José P. Zagal and Michael S. Debus, "Aligning Story and Gameplay through
Narrative Goals" (2023), https://qed.games.utah.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/cardona-rivera2023aligning.pdf ; Game
Developer, "Better Game Stories in One Step", https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/better-game-stories-in-one-step

### 16. [G] Environmental storytelling: let places and objects carry story
Henry Jenkins describes game designers as "narrative architects": story can be embedded in space and objects for the
player to find, not only spoken. Harvey Smith and Matthias Worch's GDC talk "What Happened Here?" argues that players
enjoy assembling implied events from what they see. *Gone Home* told a whole family story through objects in a house.
**Why it matters here:** the walking half of a walk-and-solve game is wasted if the places are only corridors. A
glue stick in a supply box, a mop, a shredder, a waybill on a desk: the player's errands can *be* the setup.
**Sources:** Henry Jenkins, "Game Design as Narrative Architecture" (2004),
https://web.mit.edu/~21fms/People/henry3/games&narrative.html ; Harvey Smith and Matthias Worch, "What Happened Here?
Environmental Storytelling", GDC 2010, https://gdcvault.com/play/1012647/What-Happened-Here-Environmental ; Game
Developer, "How Gone Home's design constraints lead to a powerful story",
https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-i-gone-home-i-s-design-constraints-lead-to-a-powerful-story

### 17. [G] Keep the player in the moment that matters
*Half-Life 2* was celebrated for almost never taking control away: big story moments happen around a player who can
still move and look. The general lesson isn't "no cut-scenes" but that the story's biggest moments should be the ones
the player is *present for and acting in*, not summaries read between levels.
**Why it matters here:** the beats where the player controls the lead and solves a board at the emotional peak are
the game's strongest form. Peaks that happen in narration, off-screen or to another character waste it.
**Sources:** BFI, "Half-Life 2: 20 years of Valve's game-changing shooter",
https://www.bfi.org.uk/features/half-life-2-20-years-valve-shooter ; Game Developer, "'Pick up that can': storytelling
in Half-Life 2", https://gamedeveloper.com/design/-quot-pick-up-that-can-quot-storytelling-in-half-life-2

### 18. [G] Onboarding: teach the world the way World 1-1 teaches jumping
*Super Mario Bros.*' first level teaches without text, through layout. A story game's opening has the same job for
its world: give the player one rule, one person and one problem at a time, each shown in use before the next arrives.
The opposite failure is the "proper noun word salad" critics found in *Final Fantasy XIII*, where terms (l'Cie,
fal'Cie) arrive before anyone has shown why they matter.
**Why it matters here:** this book has to onboard a stranger into three unfamiliar worlds at once: baduk, Korean
office life, and a trading company's jargon (PT, B/L, waybill, section head). Each needs to arrive at the moment it's
used, with what's at stake for the hero made clear.
**Sources:** Pinnguaq, "Mario Maker Level Design Basics" (on World 1-1), https://pinnguaq.com/learn/mario-maker-level-design-basics/ ;
Kotaku on *Final Fantasy XIII*'s terminology,
https://kotaku.com/final-fantasy-best-worst-game-rpg-square-enix-ff-16-1850567242/14

### 19. [G] Pace story against play with an interest curve
Jesse Schell suggests drawing an experience's interest over time: a hook at the start, rising peaks and valleys, and
the highest peak near the end. Calendar-driven narrative games (*Persona 5*) build a rhythm of daily life and planned
peaks around a visible clock, and critics note that relentless pace without valleys leaves no time to feel anything.
**Why it matters here:** the boards are the game's peaks. Two boards back-to-back in the middle of a confrontation, or
a beat with no board and no turn, flatten the curve. The countdown to a test is a ready-made calendar.
**Sources:** Jesse Schell, *The Art of Game Design* (Lens of the Interest Curve), reviewed at Game Developer,
https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/feature-book-review-the-art-of-game-design- ; First Person Scholar on *Persona 5*'s
calendar, https://www.firstpersonscholar.com/?p=6899 ; Kotaku, "Persona 5: The Kotaku Review",
https://kotaku.com/persona-5-the-kotaku-review-1793752909

### 20. [G] Make the deduction the player's
Shu Takumi built *Ace Attorney* around the player pointing out contradictions between testimony and evidence: the
player does the reasoning the story is about, rather than watching a detective do it. Puzzles that stand for the
character's thinking only work if the player can see what the character is thinking *about*.
**Why it matters here:** a go problem standing in for an argument or a social choice ("read the man from his game",
"hold your ground on the phone") only lands if the player can see how the stones map to the people. A metaphor board
that's opaque turns the peak into a non-sequitur.
**Sources:** "Shu Takumi", Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shu_Takumi ; Nintendo Wire, "Shu Takumi gives insight
into the creation and evolution of Ace Attorney",
https://nintendowire.com/news/2017/11/17/shu-takumi-gives-insight-creation-evolution-ace-attorney/

### 21. [G] Illustrations go where the feeling is
In a mostly-text game a full-screen image is the strongest emphasis available: it says "this moment matters". Like
environmental detail (16) and staying present (17), images should be spent on the peaks (a loss, a death, a rescue,
a reversal), not mainly on transitions and montage.
**Why it matters here:** the playtester asked for more illustrations "at key moments". Where the stills sit tells the
player where the story thinks its peaks are.
**Sources:** derived from 16 and 17 above, and from McCloud's point (13) that images and the gaps between them carry
the reader's emotion.

## Phase 2 checklist

For each beat:

1. **Link:** does it start from what the last beat caused (but / therefore)? (1, 3)
2. **Turn:** what does the lead want, what changes, and is the change felt? (2, 6)
3. **Agency:** does the lead drive the turn, and is the player controlling the one who decides? (10, 17)
4. **Board fit:** do the caption and the win line say what the scene says happened, and can the player see how the
   stones map to the moment? (14, 15, 20)
5. **Setup / payoff:** is every payoff set up earlier, and every prominent setup paid (or clearly held for later)? (4)
6. **Shown or told:** is the emotional peak dramatised, with an illustration where it counts? (8, 17, 21)
7. **Stranger test:** is every person, term and time jump introduced before it's needed, one at a time? (11, 13, 18)
8. **Order:** can a stranger tell when this happens relative to the last beat? (13)
9. **Spine:** does the beat move the book's central question forward, or is it a detour? (5, 12)
10. **Curve:** is the beat's weight (boards, stills, length) in proportion to its importance? (19)
