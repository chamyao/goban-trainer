# Editor's review: Misaeng, Book 1, "The First Move"

_Reviewed from `docs/book2/misaeng-book1-script.md` (play order), with `tools/tk_story_w21.py` checked for board
details. I haven't read the source webtoon or any research on it; this is how the book reads to a stranger. Numbers in
brackets, such as (14), refer to the principles in `docs/editor/storytelling-principles.md`._

---

## 1. Verdict

**Book 1 has a good story buried in it, and from about m14 onward it often shows.** The premise is clear and
appealing: a boy who gave his whole childhood to baduk misses turning pro by half a point, and at twenty-something has
to start again at the bottom of an office where nobody cares about baduk. The second half has a real engine: a
visible countdown to a test, a partner who can't be trusted, and a final crisis (the waybill) where an outsider is
quietly taken in by his team. "Our kid" is a moving line, and the glue-stick setup that solves the waybill is the
best piece of plotting in the book.

**But a stranger meets the book as a list, not a story, for most of its length.** The playtester's phrase, "events
are just getting thrown at me one after the next", is accurate, and the causes are easy to name:

- **The opening is about five beats of narrated backstory** (m1, m2, m4, m5 and half of m8) before the player makes a
  choice that matters in the present. Within that run the order of events is muddled: the father dies in m2, the
  "packing up" happens again in m4, and the mother is both bedridden (m2) and working building sites (m4) (7, 13).
- **The hero's biggest moments happen off-screen, in narration or to someone else.** Ahn solves his filing crisis
  (m13). He takes the blame for the waybill between beats (m21b opens with "Jang has told everyone…"). Oh does the
  detective work (m21). The bosses apologise before Jang's advice can matter (m22b). The player is present for the
  setup of each turn and absent for its peak (10, 17).
- **Three boards tell the player that solving them means failing** (m2, m17, m18). This is the clearest single
  craft fault in the book, because it teaches the player that their own success is meaningless (14).
- **After m19 the book loses its spine.** The partner conflict ends in m19. The waybill (m20–m21b) is a strong
  standalone story but isn't tied to the PT or to Han, and m22–m22b turns to a dispute between new senior characters
  the player has never met. The book ends on someone else's apology and a sauna gag rather than on Jang (5, 12).

**Is it clear?** Mostly line by line; not beat to beat. **Gripping?** In stretches: m8, m17–m19 and m20–m21b. **Coherent?**
The world is, but the timeline of the prologue and the question of who knows baduk aren't. **Does it have a shape?** It
has a strong *frame* (the countdown, the title idea of "misaeng" returning in the last line) but not yet a *climax*
that the hero drives. Fix the opening's order, put Jang in the middle of the waybill, and make the boards agree with
the story, and this becomes a good book with very few new beats.

| m14–m19 | Finding a partner for the PT and standing up to him | Jang, Han | The PT; not being used | **The book's strongest spine.** Resolved at m19. |

| m22–m22b | Office politics over a dog-meat dinner | Kim Bu-ryeon, Steve Han, Go | Sales Team 1's approvals | New characters at the climax; Jang's advice is made moot. |
| Closing card | "He still isn't alive on this board" | Jang | The PT | A good image, but the book hasn't put Jang under pressure since m21b. |

**What a stranger would say it's about:** "A failed baduk player becomes an intern and has to pass a test; along
the way he learns the office is a kind of baduk too." That's a sound throughline. What's missing is **one question the
whole book is pushing at**. The promise of m8 ("pass the PT or be exposed as a parachute") is the right one, but the
book never shows anyone *calling* him a parachute, never shows his PT work being judged by anyone but Han, and lets
the PT fade from m20 onwards. Meanwhile **his mother**, the stake set up in m4–m5 ("a mother to look after"), drops
out entirely after the tie scene.

## 3. Beat-by-beat notes

### Opening card
- **Works:** sets a tone of seriousness and a clock ("145 moves"). "A small boy who can't leave them alone" is a nice
  hook.
- **Doesn't:** the frame is never explained to a stranger. Who are Nie Weiping and Cho Hunhyun to Jang? Cho turns up
  again only in the "which move did Cho play?" quizzes and as a name in a dream (m19c). Without a reason, the frame
  reads as a separate game bolted onto the story (4, 18). The closing card's "Sixteen moves played" assumes the player
  has been tracking it.

### m1: Atari
- **Works:** a clean, playable first board ("Find the atari") that a beginner can solve, said by a child, matching the
  scene (14, 18). The uncle's "Go on, then" is a warm invitation. The swallowed stones are a vivid, specific detail.
- **Doesn't:** after the board, four stills of montage and a block of narration skim several years. It's all told
  (8). "Mr. Kim at the laundromat" adds a fourth Kim to the book for no payoff (11). The most important fact in the
  beat, that **his father's company went under**, is hidden in a clause about a sedative simile. It's a setup for the
  family's poverty and the father's death, and it doesn't register as one (4).
- The opening line defines *misaeng* before the player has any reason to care. It pays off beautifully in the book's
  last line, but here it's a dictionary entry. Consider moving the definition to the moment he first sees an
  unsettled group of his own (m2's lost game, or m13).
- The "Seven Years Later" card repeats the narration that comes just before it ("At eleven he joined the trainees'
  room") and then m2 repeats "seven years" again.

### m2: Thrown Away
- **Works:** this has the book's best prose ("He hasn't changed; everyone else has", the stones dropped from his
  pocket). The half-point idea is now explained before it's used, which fixes a playtest complaint. The self-blame
  ("he tells himself he just didn't try hard enough") is a strong, specific wound for the hero to carry (6).
- **Doesn't:**
  - **The board contradicts the story.** The caption is "Win by half a point"; Jang thinks "Not this time"; the player
    solves it; the win line is "The last stone"; and then: "Half a point short. It's over." The player's success is
    turned into failure with no explanation (14, 15). The playtester's "half a point… again" confusion probably lives
    here: the player can't tell whether they did something wrong.
  - **"For seven years he has lost that way, over and over"** is told, not shown (8). The player never sees a single
    earlier half-point loss, so "again" has nothing to echo.
  - **The father's death is one line in a paragraph of summary** ("Not long after, his father died, and his mother
    took to her bed.") with no still and no reaction (8, 21). This is the playtest note "a father's death never
    told": it's technically told, but nothing in the presentation tells the player it matters.
  - **Order of events is confusing** (13). The beat runs: loss → father dies → mother bedridden → "the day he leaves"
    → reasons including "the part-time jobs" (never mentioned yet) → he walks away. Did he leave the trainees' room
    before or after his father died? Did the part-time jobs come before? A stranger can't tell.
  - Lee Chang-ho and Lee Sedol are named without context. Fine as flavour, but it's two more names in the first two
    beats.

### m4: Washing Her Back
- **Works:** "In the bathroom he washed her back" is a strong, physical image of care and the right still for it (8,
  21). The mother's line handing him to the sponsor is a clean cause-and-effect link into m5 (1).
- **Doesn't:**
  - **It steps back in time** to "When he packed up" and putting his records out with the rubbish, which m2 already
  covered with the stones. A stranger reads this as a second departure (13).
  - **"The family restaurant failed"** is new. In m1 it was "his father's company". Was the restaurant after the
  company? Is it the same business? (4, 13)
  - **The mother is bedridden in m2 and working building sites "until her body gave out" here.** Probably m4 explains
  m2 (she worked, then collapsed), but in play order it reads as a contradiction.
  - "Then he did his military service" plus a "Years Later" card: two time jumps in two lines.
  - "Your old sponsor… the man who paid for all your baduk": in m1 the mother paid. The sponsor needs one line of
  setup in m1 (the dojang), or this reads as a new fact convenient to the plot (4).
- No board. This is acceptable for a sequel beat (3), but combined with m5 and m7, the player goes three beats without
  touching a stone.

### m5: A Light Allowed Me
- **Works:** "parachute" is a clean, concrete stake, introduced with a definition at the moment it matters (6, 18).
  Cutting to Oh on a mountain with his sons, having forgotten the meeting Jang will be thrown into, is the book's best
  use of dramatic irony: the player knows these two will collide before either does (9). The mother tying his tie is
  a lovely, quiet image.
- **Doesn't:** "If there's a light I'm meant to keep burning, I'll take responsibility for it. But is there a light
  allowed for someone like me?" Without the source, this line has no referent. Lights of the office windows? A
  metaphor from somewhere? It gives the beat its title, so it should mean something to a stranger (13).
- The "Department head (phone)" is never identified. Is this Kim Bu-ryeon, who appears in m22 running Sales Teams 1
  to 3? A stranger can't link them (11).

### m7: Fear Is Rational
- **Works:** the timed walk (the engine's clock) matches the story: Oh is racing to be less late. That's a small piece
  of ludonarrative harmony (14). "This fear is completely rational" is a good comic line and establishes Oh as funny,
  harried and honest.
- **Doesn't:** it's a whole beat for a joke that the m8 café scene already implies. Nothing turns for Jang (2). The
  "workshop" set-up line is weak; the joke works better if Oh has said "fear is irrational" himself earlier, perhaps on
  the mountain (4).

### m8: Baduk, Not Go
- **Works:** this is the book's first real *scene* (goal: keep the buyer; conflict: he can't talk trade; turn: he can
  talk baduk) (3). The snapback puzzle given to the buyer is perfect board-fit: Jang literally gives one stone to win
  more, and the player does what Jang does (14, 15). The title, "Baduk, Not Go", pays off in the buyer's question. Oh
  bursting in, in hiking clothes, pays off m5 and m7 (4). The "sloppy, red-eyed trainee" memory is a smart way to let
  baduk be Jang's lens on people.
- **Doesn't:**
  - **Too much for one beat** (C2 in the team's own playbook; 19): café scene, car ride, reading Oh, HR assignment,
    PT stakes, Kim's introduction and a first errand. The central dramatic question of the whole book, "pass the PT
    in two months or everyone will know he was a parachute", arrives here as one paragraph between other events.
  - **"Read the man from his game"** is a metaphor board the player can't map (20). The scene is Jang reading Oh's
    character; the board is a random tesuji. What does solving it have to do with being "obsessive and responsible"?
  - It's unclear whether Oh knew about the quiz or was grateful. His reaction to Jang saving the meeting, which is the
    start of their relationship, is missing (2).

### m10: Who Do You Think You Are?
- **Works:** the filing conflict is very good. It's the hero's *strength* (working alone, his own system) shown as a
  flaw in a new world ("This isn't work you do alone; it's work we do together"). That's the book's theme in one line
  (6). The memory of filing his game records his own way at fifteen is a precise, earned link (4). **The glue stick
  in the supply box is a superb plant** for m20–m21 (4, 16).
- **Doesn't:**
  - No board at the peak. The moment Kim says "Who do you think you are?" is the turn, and the player only walked an
    errand. A short board here (a group that lives only with its neighbour) would make the theme playable (17).
  - "We're going to work out who the dud is" introduces "dud" without saying it means the weakest intern, the one you
    want as a PT partner so you look good. That idea is only explained in m14 (18).

### m12: Secure Yourself First
- **Works:** Ahn's introduction is efficient and memorable: top of the intake, the only woman, and she acts (catches
  his sleeve) before she speaks (11). The proverb board fits the moment exactly: secure your own group, then attack
  (14). "He'd lived by that proverb his whole life, and he had to hear it from someone else" is a good, rueful turn
  (2). Ahn making the glasses intern apologise is a clean causal hand-off (1).
- **Doesn't:**
  - **Does Ahn play baduk?** She quotes a go proverb word for word, later explains seki (m13), and gives go-shaped
    advice again in m16 and m19c. But m22b makes a point of Jang grinning when Oh uses a "baduk word, from someone who
    doesn't play". If nobody in the office plays, Ahn's proverbs need a reason; if Ahn plays, that's a character fact
    worth one line (11).
  - "Pull yourself together… You haven't said a word all night. You won't learn just by listening" and then the
    filing point: three different pieces of advice in a row. The filing one is the one that matters.

### m13: Both Live
- **Works:** seki ("both live") is now explained in one sentence before the board, which addresses the playtest note
  (18). The board fits the scene exactly: two systems that don't need to kill each other (14). Kim's grudging "...Good
  idea" is a satisfying small turn. "She kept her own group alive, and his too" is a lovely line.
- **Doesn't:**
  - **The hero's problem is solved by someone else**, and the player is handed Ahn to do it (10, 17). Jang ran back to
    the office in m12 to fix his filing; his own decision should be what fixes it. As written he's a bystander at his
    own payoff.
  - The "25 stones" dream (rows of white stones and one black) is a striking still with no payoff in this book. If
    it's paid in Book 2, fine; if not, it's a loose gun (4). A stranger can't tell whether the black stone is Jang
    among the white-collar workers or something else.
  - Minor: the beat ends "goes to Susaek-dong" (home, to dream), and m14 starts at Sales 3.

### m14: The World Is Faster
- **Works:** the countdown ("Seven weeks to the PT") arrives and becomes the book's best structural device (5, 19).
  Kim's "if you pair with a sure dud, you shine… Be careful of whoever comes to you first" is a crisp setup that Han
  pays off two beats later (4, 9). The three errands make the office tangible (16), and "Better plain and on time than
  perfect and late" carries m10's lesson forward (1).
- **Doesn't:**
  - **"Which move did Cho Hunhyun play?"** has nothing to do with this scene (15). Jang's thought ("Black approaches,
    and the hard fight starts here") is a stretch. The quiz interrupts the transition from the rush-hour still to the
    PT news.
  - "The forwarder call about the B/L" is jargon in an objective: what's a B/L or a forwarder? (18) Say "bill of
    lading" once, or call it "the shipping papers".
  - Ahn announces the PT details. It would be stronger from HR or Oh, and it adds to Ahn's role as the explainer of
    everything.

### m16: Inside the Board
- **Works:** the onlookers' still is a good image for the theme (you can't see your own mistakes; everyone else can).
  Ahn's "trust them" sets up a dilemma against Kim's "be careful" (9).
- **Doesn't:**
  - **Nothing happens.** No event, no turn (2). It's six weeks out and he has no partner at the start and at the end;
    Han's invitation at the end is the only event.
  - **Is the ringed trainee a memory or a present-day scene?** On the forecourt in Jongno? The text doesn't say (13).
  - "See what the watchers see" is another board the player can't map to the story (20).
  - Ahn gives a second go-shaped lecture in four beats.

### m17: Sente
- **Works:** Han is introduced well: a self-intro, a reason he's here (back from Ulsan for the PT), and a vivid
  trait (credentials "slammed down by the handful") (11). "Why did you choose me?" is the right question, and leaving
  it unanswered is a good gap (9), because Kim's warning in m14 hands the player the probable answer (he's after a
  dud) while Jang doesn't see it. The Ulsan mistake is a clean plant.
- **Doesn't:**
  - **The board contradicts the story again.** Caption: "Take sente." Jang: "Lead this time." The player solves it.
    Win line: "Han snatches it back with both hands." The player succeeds and is told they failed (14).
  - Ahn and the glasses intern talk on the roof at night after Han's offer, as if they overheard. Where are they? (13)
  - "Bomb squad" and "nuclear bomb" (m18) are slang layered on "dud" without explanation.

### m18: Again!
- **Works:** the title line, Han texting back a single word, is funny and sharp (the illustration fits). The cut to
  Han being told at Ulsan that this is his last chance is excellent dramatic irony: the player now knows Han is
  desperate, and Jang doesn't (9). "Beside an abandoned board" is a good closing image.
- **Doesn't:**
  - **Third contradicting board.** "Hold your ground on the phone"; the player wins; the win line is Han saying "Find
    it yourself". Jang didn't hold his ground (14).
  - "After a board where you fight alone, the world seems kinder and warmer" is hard to parse. Is it saying that
    teamwork feels kinder after baduk's solitude? (13)
  - Ahn's "Maybe he's a big dud. A nuclear bomb." It's unclear whether she means Jang or Han, and it seems out of
    character for the person who has been looking out for him (11).
  - The phone confrontation happens in two short lines and isn't really a scene.

### m19: How Old Are You?
- **Works:** this is the book's best payoff for the hero. He finally takes sente, and "he was raised to compete, and he
  doesn't hand over sente" pays off m17's promise (4, 5). "Take the PT back" fits its board well (14). "And how old are
  you, anyway?" is a great button, and Han's "This little runt…" lands.
- **Doesn't:**
  - **The logic of the turn is odd.** Han finally approves an idea ("The second one's good"), and *then* Jang
    rebels. Why rebel at the moment he gets what he wanted? The intent is presumably that Jang seizes control so it
    can't happen again, but a stranger needs one line saying so (1).
  - **A history quiz sits in the middle of the confrontation** ("Which move did Cho play? Black 11"), between Han's
    approval and Jang's stand. It breaks the scene's tension at its peak (19).
  - "How old are you?" relies on the Korean convention that age sets rank and speech level. A stranger may read it as
    random rudeness. One line of narration ("In Korea, age decides who speaks down to whom; Han is older") would make
    the joke land.
  - Oh's criticism of the document ("Shrink the boxes") is set up nowhere and paid off nowhere.

### m19c: The Eye of the Storm
- **Works:** the overheard conversation is good irony. Oh and Kim think Jang's being used just after he has stopped
  being used (9). The fading heroes are an affecting image of letting go of baduk.
- **Doesn't:**
  - **"The people who use him, help him, get angry with him and scold him are all on his side. Here, he's not an
    outsider."** This *tells* the emotional payoff that m21b is built to *show* (8). By the time Oh says "our kid",
    the book has already told us he belongs.
  - Ahn's tornado metaphor is her fourth piece of proverb-advice. The "eye of the storm" idea isn't paid off in this
    book (Han and Jang don't reach his calm centre).
  - Four go players' names in one line (Cho Nam-chul, Cho Hunhyun, Lee Chang-ho, Lee Sedol) (11).
  - No board in a beat about letting go of baduk. Missed chance: let the player play out a hero's famous move as it
    fades (17).

### m20: The Waybill
- **Works:** the plotting is excellent. Kim's shredding rule (setup), Jang's small mistake of leaving it for later, the
  locked cabinet, the glue, the yellow sheet sliding off the security desk: a clean chain of "therefore" (1, 4). The
  cross-cut to the lobby while Jang hums at the shredder is Hitchcock's bomb under the table done right (9).
- **Doesn't:**
  - **The crisis for Jang is never shown.** "The interns get yelled at on the roof" is one sentence. We never see Jang
    realise the waybill is gone, own up, or get blamed (8, 17). This is the scene the playtester meant: "a rescue that
    didn't land because the bullying wasn't shown."
  - **No board**, even though this is the climax of the book. There's a natural one: the player chooses to step
    forward and take the blame (sacrifice a stone) (14).
  - "Director! Is this about a team dinner?" is a puzzling line; presumably Oh is being nervously jokey.
  - The stakes aren't linked to the PT. "The interns are graded on everything" is good; make it explicit that the
    waybill could end Jang's chance (6).

### m21: A Secret
- **Works:** the clue board makes the player reason through the plants (glue, key, scrap) themselves. This is the
  book's best ludonarrative moment: the deduction is the player's (16, 20). Go is introduced vividly, and his speech
  about his intern (newly married, eldest son of an eldest son) gives the culprit a reason to hide that the player
  can understand.
- **Doesn't:**
  - **It's Oh's beat, not Jang's.** That's justified: the point is Oh's care for Jang, and the player learning how
    the mentor thinks. But combined with m20's missing blame scene, Jang drops out of his own climax (10).
  - "An intern from another team let Jang take the blame for the waybill. Oh means to find out how" gives away the
    deduction before the player does it (9). Let the clue board reveal it.

### m21b: Our Kid
- **Works:** "Our kid" is the emotional peak of the book and earns the beat's title. Oh refusing to name the culprit
  ("That's a secret") and then blurting it out under provocation is funny and in character. Kim Seok-ho's "...I'm
  sorry" and the baby's hand round his finger turn the culprit into a person (it's the book's kindest moment).
- **Doesn't:**
  - **"Jang has told everyone the waybill was his fault"** is the hero's most important choice in the book, and it
    happens off-screen and is reported in a stage direction (8, 10, 17). Without it, "our kid" is a rescue from a
    danger the player never felt.
  - No still for "our kid" itself; the still goes to the baby instead (21). Both are good; "our kid" matters more.
  - "It isn't even nine before he's tipsy" is a nice touch.

### m22: Their Own Baduk
- **Works:** the harbour visit plants a real lesson (go and look at the site), and Steve Han's speech ("A fat report is
  just a way for nobody to take responsibility") is the book's sharpest statement of what office work values. "Dog
  meat" is a great comic reveal.
- **Doesn't:**
  - **New senior characters at the book's end** (Steve Han and Kim Bu-ryeon, both unintroduced; Steve shares a
    surname with Han Seok-yul) (11). The beat's conflict isn't Jang's.
  - **The harbour trip is narrated** (8) and has no consequence in the book.
  - **Another history quiz** with a caption unrelated to the moment (15).
  - "Everyone is playing their own game of baduk" is the theme said out loud (8).

### m22b: Dead Stones
- **Works:** "Give up the dead stone" is a strong, well-fitted board (14), and "you don't leave dead stones lying on the
  board… it grows into a bigger problem" is a good piece of baduk wisdom that actually applies. "Knowing when to bow
  your head: that's class" is a strong line for Oh. The last line ("He still isn't alive on this board. But for the
  first time, he can see how a group like his might live") closes the *misaeng* idea opened in m1. That's a good
  bookend (4, 5).
- **Doesn't:**
  - **"Checkmate" is a chess term, not a baduk term.** "A baduk word, from someone who doesn't play" will confuse
    anyone who knows either game (13). If the Korean is a janggi term, translate it as such, or use a real baduk word.
  - **Jang's advice is made moot.** The bosses are already apologising when the team arrives. That might be the
    intended irony (the obvious move was obvious to everyone), but in a game it means the player's solution changed
    nothing (10, 14).
  - The sauna still and the "Don't touch me!" button are funny but end the book on minor characters.
  - "Two weeks to the PT" appears both here and in m22, so the countdown stalls at the end.

### Closing card
- "A partner who no longer gives the orders" and "a team that's on his side" are both earned. "A man with a
  resignation letter in his jacket, a child at a daycare door" is a good hook for Book 2.

## 4. Top ten problems, ranked

### 1. The hero's peaks happen off-screen or to someone else
**Where:** m20–m21b (the blame), m13 (filing), m22b (apology), m21 (investigation).
**Why it hurts:** the player never experiences the danger that "our kid" rescues him from, so the climax lands as
information (8, 10, 17).
**Fix (no new beats):** in m20, replace "The interns get yelled at on the roof" with the roof scene itself, played as
Jang: Oh asks whose waybill it is; the player solves a board captioned *"Own the mistake"* (a sacrifice problem: give
up a stone to save the group); Jang steps forward ("It was mine. I left it on my desk."), and the others look at
him. Add a still. Then m21's Oh investigation becomes a reaction to what the player has just seen Jang do, and m21b's
"Jang has told everyone" line can go. In m13, let Jang propose the cross-index and give Ahn the line that gets Kim to
listen, or give the board to Jang.

### 2. Boards that say the player's success is failure
**Where:** m2 ("Win by half a point" → loses), m17 ("Take sente" → Han takes it), m18 ("Hold your ground" → he doesn't).
**Why it hurts:** it's ludonarrative dissonance in its plainest form (14). It also creates the "half a point… again"
confusion: the player can't tell whether they made a mistake.
**Fix:** rewrite the caption and win line so solving = what actually happens. m2: *"Count the game."* The player
counts the final position correctly and the answer is the loss: "Half a point." (The truth he has to see is the
puzzle.) Or caption *"Play it perfectly"* with the win line *"Perfect, and still half a point short."* m17: *"Answer
Han's move"*, win line *"He keeps sente anyway."* (The player plays the best reply and still loses the initiative,
which is the point.) m18: *"Find what Han wants"*, win line *"Find it yourself."* Keep "Take sente" for m19, where Jang
actually does.

### 3. The prologue is long, told and out of order
**Where:** m1, m2, m4.
**Why it hurts:** five beats of summary before the present story; the father's death and the mother's collapse are
buried; m4 jumps back in time; the business that failed changes from "company" to "restaurant" (7, 8, 13).
**Fix:** put everything in strict time order and give each beat one fact (the playbook's C2):
- m1: childhood, ending with the father's company failing *as its own sentence*, with the sponsor named at the dojang.
- m2: the half-point loss (board), the walk out dropping stones, the records in the rubbish (move from m4). End there.
- m4: the father's death as the beat's opening event, with its own still (a funeral portrait, or his empty chair), then
  the restaurant (introduced as what the family tried next), the mother on building sites, her collapse, the bath,
  the army. The self-blame paragraph ("he tells himself he just didn't try hard enough") fits better here, after the
  death, than in m2.
- Cut the "Seven Years Later" card's repeated narration.

### 4. After m19 the book has no spine
**Where:** m20–m22b.
**Why it hurts:** the PT partner story resolves, and the waybill and dog-meat episodes read as "and then" (1, 5, 12).
The ending belongs to minor characters.
**Fix:** tie each late beat back to the PT and to Jang. m20: an HR or Oh line that a disciplinary mark goes into the
intern file before the PT, so the waybill could cost Jang his place. m21b: Oh's "Stay on your toes" can mention the PT.
m22b: let Jang's insight be what reaches Oh *before* the bosses apologise (Oh hurries upstairs because of it, and
arrives to see it already happening), and close on Jang applying the dead-stone lesson to his own PT (dropping an
idea he loves, or something he must apologise to Han for). Bring the mother back in one line (a text from home, the
tie) so the stake from m4–m5 is alive at the end.

### 5. The Ing Cup frame and its history quizzes are unexplained and interrupt scenes
**Where:** opening card, m14, m19, m22, closing card.
**Why it hurts:** "Which move did Cho Hunhyun play?" has no connection to the moment, and in m19 it breaks the
confrontation at its peak (15, 19). A stranger doesn't know why this game matters.
**Fix:** in the opening card, give one reason this game belongs to Jang (it's the game his parents kept a clipping of;
Cho is his childhood hero, whom he names in m19c). Move quiz boards to the *start* of their beats, as a breath before
the scene, never in the middle. Give each quiz a caption tied to the beat: m14 *"Cho approaches early. Do you?"*,
m19 *"Cho makes his corner solid before he fights. Where?"*, m22 *"Cho prepared for this. Which move?"*.

### 6. Too many similar names and unidentified roles
**Where:** throughout. Four Kims (Mr. Kim at the laundromat, Kim Dong-sik, Kim Seok-ho, Kim Bu-ryeon), two Hans (Han
Seok-yul, Steve Han), Oh and Go, an unnamed "Department head (phone)", an unnamed "glasses intern", "Kang".
**Why it hurts:** a stranger can't keep them apart, especially in a language they don't know (11).
**Fix:** drop "Mr. Kim" in m1 ("a neighbour at the laundromat"). Give each Kim a fixed epithet on screen: "Assistant
Manager Kim" (Dong-sik), "Kim Seok-ho, the intern from Team 1", "Department Head Kim" (Bu-ryeon). Identify the phone
voice in m5 as that same department head. Call Steve Han only "Steve". Introduce Go with his team every time until
m21b.

### 7. The belonging payoff is told before it's shown
**Where:** m19c ("Here, he's not an outsider") before m21b ("Our kid").
**Why it hurts:** m21b's emotion is spent in advance (4, 8).
**Fix:** cut the m19c sentence and make the team dinner ambivalent: he downs the penalty drinks, laughs along, and still
feels like a guest. Then "our kid" is the first time he's claimed. Also give "our kid" a still.

### 8. Who plays baduk, and "checkmate"
**Where:** Ahn's proverbs (m12, m13, m16, m19c) vs. m22b's "a baduk word, from someone who doesn't play"; "checkmate".
**Why it hurts:** a contradiction in the world's rules (13), and a factual error a go player will notice immediately.
**Fix:** decide whether Ahn plays. If she does, one line in m12 ("My grandfather played") makes her proverbs natural and
makes her a mirror for Jang. If she doesn't, give her own metaphors and let Jang translate them into baduk in his
head. In m22b, either translate the Korean term properly or have Oh say something that sounds like baduk by accident
("dead in the water" → Jang thinks "dead stones").

### 9. Overloaded beats next to empty ones
**Where:** m8 (café, car, Oh, HR, PT stakes, Kim) and m22 (harbour, Steve, quiz, dog meat) are crowded; m7 and m16 have
no turn.
**Why it hurts:** the central question of the book (the PT) arrives buried in m8, and the interest curve has no
valleys or peaks where they're needed (2, 19).
**Fix:** move the HR assignment, the PT stakes and Kim's introduction from m8 to the opening of m10, so m8 ends on the
café triumph and the car. In m16, give the empty beat its event: the other interns pair up in front of him, and he's
left alone (a still), which makes Han's offer land. Fold Oh's "fear is irrational" setup into m5's summit so m7's joke
has its own plant.

### 10. Illustrations are front-loaded and miss the peaks
**Where:** m1 has four montage stills. Undrawn peaks: the half-point count, the father's death, Oh bursting into the
café, the moment Jang owns the waybill, "our kid", Ahn's both-live solution.
**Why it hurts:** the stills tell the player where the story's weight is, and they currently point at transitions (21).
**Fix:** cut m1's montage to two stills (class, trainees' room). Spend the freed stills on: the count in m2 (the
board with a half-point margin written in), the father's death in m4, Oh in hiking clothes at the café in m8, Jang
stepping forward on the roof in m20, and Oh's "our kid" in m21b.

**Also worth fixing, below the line:** metaphor boards the player can't map (m8 "Read the man", m16 "See what the
watchers see") (20); the unexplained "How old are you?" convention (18); "dud", "B/L" and "bomb squad" introduced
before they're explained (18); the "25 stones" dream and the "eye of the storm" with no payoff in this book (4); and
"Is there a light allowed for someone like me?" (13).

## 5. What's working (protect these)

- **The premise and the hero's wound.** A prodigy who lost by half a point and tells himself he "just didn't try hard
  enough" is a specific, sympathetic engine (6).
- **The PT countdown.** "Eight weeks… seven… six…" is cheap, clear progress and a natural calendar (5, 19).
- **The waybill plotting**: glue stick (m10) → shredding rule and locked cabinet (m20) → the lobby cross-cut → the
  clue board (m21). Setup and payoff at its best, and the clue board lets the player do the deduction (4, 9, 20).
- **Oh's introduction** on the mountain, the traffic clock, and the hiking clothes at the café: dramatic irony that
  pays off within three beats (9).
- **The café snapback puzzle**: the clearest example in the book of a board meaning exactly what the scene means (14).
- **Boards that fit:** m1 atari, m8 snapback, m12 secure yourself first, m13 both live, m19 take the PT back, m22b give
  up the dead stone. These are the model for the rest.
- **Baduk as Jang's lens on people** (the sloppy trainee he misread; Han's credentials as stones slammed down by the
  handful). Keep this voice.
- **Kim Dong-sik's "This isn't work you do alone; it's work we do together"**: the theme, stated by a character with a
  grudge, at the right moment.
- **Han's Ulsan cut-away** (m18): the player learns he's desperate before Jang does.
- **"Our kid"**, Oh's "That's a secret", and the baby's hand round Kim Seok-ho's finger.
- **The prose of m2** ("He hasn't changed; everyone else has"; dropping stones from his pocket).
- **The bookend**: *misaeng* defined in m1, and "he still isn't alive on this board" in the last line.
- **"How old are you, anyway?" / "This little runt…"**: a great button, once the age convention is explained.

## 6. Questions for the story writer

1. **The opening frame:** why this game (Nie vs. Cho, 1989)? Does it connect to Jang's life or to the half-point
   theme, and will the player be told?
2. **m2's board:** is the player meant to experience the half-point loss as *their* failure, or as something they
   couldn't prevent? The caption should follow from the answer.
3. **Timeline of m1–m4:** what's the real order of the father's company failing, the restaurant, the trainee
   deadline, the father's death, the mother's collapse, the part-time jobs and the army?
4. **The sponsor:** when did he start paying, and should he appear in m1?
5. **"A light allowed me":** what is the light? Is it a recurring image in later books?
6. **The department head on the phone in m5:** is that Kim Bu-ryeon?
7. **Does Ahn play baduk?** Is her proverb-giving meant to be a character trait, or narrative convenience?
8. **The 25 stones dream** (m13) and **"the eye of the tornado"** (m19c): are these paid off in Book 2?
9. **Why does Han choose Jang?** Is it the "dud" strategy Kim warned about? Will the player find out?
10. **Why does Jang rebel in m19** right after Han approves an idea?
11. **Why does Jang take the blame for the waybill?** Does he believe it was his fault (he did leave it on his desk),
    or is he protecting someone? The answer decides how m20 should be played.
12. **"Checkmate" in m22b:** what's the Korean term, and is the joke that Oh used a chess (or janggi) word?
13. **Was the bosses' apology in m22b meant to make Jang's advice moot** (irony), or to confirm it?
14. **The mother:** does she return in Book 2? If so, a line in Book 1's last beats would keep her present.
15. **The "glasses intern":** does he get a name later? He drives two beats (m10, m12) and the "dud" thread.
